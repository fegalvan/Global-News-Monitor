# Following up on the news relevance audit

Opening an original link from the Vietnam coverage page exposed two event records that looked like a diplomatic visit. The publisher had posted an obituary, and Vietnam appeared in the account of Gary Sunderman’s military service. The original GDELT export already contained the two visit codes. Other cases showed a separate problem in GNM. A report about a breach of wage law could end up in Cyber, and a political appeal could end up in Crisis. [News relevance audit](NEWS_RELEVANCE_AUDIT.md)

The follow-up tightens those category rules, recovers available context, and makes the original links easier to inspect. All results below come from isolated local copies of the same real GDELT snapshot. The release candidate has not been deployed.

## Checking the category rules on new articles

The Cyber rules no longer infer a cyber incident from “breach” or the generic intelligence-sharing code `064`. Crisis requires supporting context for topics such as wildfires, floods, and outbreaks. Generic appeals for aid, political reform, or yielding now use their CAMEO root when no more specific context is available. The review also moved CAMEO root `15` from Protest to Conflict & military. The broad root group covering statements, appeals, and consultation is now called Statements & diplomacy. Action labels now use the pinned CAMEO dictionary with a few concise equivalents. This removes old translations that treated `071` economic aid as humanitarian aid and `172` administrative sanctions as a military attack. [Classification method](TECHNICAL_WALKTHROUGH.md#classification) · [GDELT Events codebook](https://data.gdeltproject.org/documentation/GDELT-Event_Codebook-V2.0.pdf)

The first held-out pass used 54 links distinct from the audit. The first revision correctly assigned 24 of 30 independently scorable links to Cyber, Crisis, or neither, up from 15. It removed 15 false topical assignments and missed six relevant stories the old version had picked up. Those misses guided a small second pass on phrases such as “network breach” and “floodwaters.” The final rules were frozen before publisher content was reviewed for the second holdout.

The final sample used another 54 links from 52 hostnames and 25 coded countries. It deliberately overrepresented records affected by the rule changes. A deterministic draw excluded URL identities from the audit and first sample. Publisher text was checked before comparing the labels with the final results. Of the 54 links, 31 had enough relevant, distinct article text to score for Cyber, Crisis, or neither. The other 23 were military/protest controls, overlapped an earlier story, or had ambiguous or unavailable publisher text. Three reports on a breach of US intelligence-agency systems were also counted once in a separate sensitivity check.

| Topic check on the final holdout | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Correct among 31 links | 14 | 24 |
| Unrelated articles assigned to Cyber | 8 | 0 |
| Unrelated articles assigned to Crisis | 9 | 2 |
| Relevant Cyber stories assigned to Cyber | 9 of 9 | 6 of 9 |
| Relevant Crisis stories assigned to Crisis | 5 of 5 | 3 of 5 |
| Correct when scoring the shared story once | 12 of 29 | 23 of 29 |

The sample supports a reduction in false labels for the specific rules tested. It cannot estimate error rates across GDELT or across languages. Some real stories still needed context the URL and actors did not provide. The new rules missed a Korean brokerage’s data breach and a Fiji drought report, and still interpreted “blue tsunami” in two US election stories as a crisis. The military and protest controls also illustrated why mapping the root correctly cannot fix a wrong GDELT action. Varied sources is the UI default. It balances hostnames and uses the availability of code, actors, and known location precision to break ties within a recent hour. Country-level and local precision both add context. Neither exact coordinates nor additional records for the same URL get a special boost. Most recent keeps the old order. Both modes have the same inspection counts and URL grouping, and both lead back to the underlying records. The API still defaults to Most recent for existing callers.

These checks measure source variety and visible record context. They do not measure editorial relevance or independence of sources. Different hostnames can publish the same syndicated article. Named actors, affiliations, and precise-looking labels can still be wrong. The dashboard makes no additional requests to publishers.

In the audit’s ten same-slot Mentions exports, all 170 checked event IDs had a mention matching the original URL. Only nine had a second URL from another host. The obituary also had confidence 100, while two useful reports had mentions at confidence 20. Mentions could supply alternative links in a future bounded trial across later slots. This sample supports keeping them out of the live ranking for now. Coded actor countries can help a reader spot a mismatch, but a record's action geography and actor country need not match. ## Direct counts and effect on comparisons

The migration updated 4,887 normalized category assignments or associated rule details out of 251,158 retained records. Full-row raw fingerprints and all normalized identities matched before and after. SQL found zero differences between direct retained counts and the hourly live rollups by UTC hour, country, category, and map availability. Historical counts also matched the live counts in this snapshot, which had 234 completed export slots and no archive-only contributions.

Both comparison days ended at 03:00 UTC, on November 24 and November 25, 2026. Each day had all 96 expected exports. The worldwide change remained **108,817 → 114,228 (+5,411, 4.97%)**. ## Migration recovery and archive limits

The parser now retains action geography type and both actor-country fields from new files. In a fresh local database, the official 02:45 UTC export on November 25, 2026 inserted 807 records. All 807 raw payloads contained the three keys, the direct and both aggregate counts matched, and replay added zero records.

The local archive rehearsal restored context for 251,158 of 251,158 retained events from 234 original exports. There were no identity mismatches, ambiguous rows, missing files, or coordinates to clear in this snapshot. The source supplied an actor-country value for 141,484 first actors and 110,875 second actors. Blank values stay unknown. The supplemental rows leave the original stored JSON intact.

A later rollout can rehearse the migration against a verified backup with writers paused. For each backfill range, run the bounded dry run first, inspect the match totals, then repeat with `--apply` when ready. The following command is an example for an isolated copy. [Backfill script](../scripts/backfill_gdelt_context.py)

```sh
python scripts/backfill_gdelt_context.py \
  --start 2026-11-25T00:00:00Z --end 2026-11-25T03:00:00Z --max-exports 12
```

The old preview database and its backup were preserved. The migration was also downgraded on the working copy. A checksum of every restored normalized category tuple matched the original, and the final migration was then reapplied. Integration fixtures verify that the downgrade preserves archive counts and restores any normalized coordinates corrected by a type `0` match. Revision `0012_category_quality` checks that no subsequent ingestion or pruning has invalidated its local undo data. If that check fails, recovery should use the pre-upgrade backup and reconcile subsequent exports before switching traffic.

Aggregate-only hours are different. Once the 17-day raw detail expires, the 90-day archive keeps the counts without the original event identities or URLs. Original files alone cannot reliably reconstruct which rows survived ingestion and deduplication. The code preserves those earlier counts, hides category comparisons that would span rule definitions, and withholds older category values from category charts and anomaly baselines. The cutoff is the first hour after the last archive-only contribution and depends on the database at migration time. A PostgreSQL fixture exercised that case. The local snapshot had no such cutoff. Production’s cutoff remains to be measured on a restored copy.

## Checks, latency, and footprint

The regression run passed 116 Python tests, including real PostgreSQL migration and backfill fixtures, and 9 frontend tests. TypeScript, the Vite production build, and the installed Python dependency check passed. Chromium checked desktop and mobile layouts, map zoom and reset, country navigation and copied filters, original links, record pagination, both sort orders, loading and retry behavior, and empty results.

The local API benchmarks used a warmed database and 10 timed requests per endpoint and version over loopback.
